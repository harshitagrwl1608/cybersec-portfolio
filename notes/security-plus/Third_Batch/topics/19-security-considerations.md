# Security Considerations: Regulatory, Legal, Industry, and Geographic

**CompTIA Security+ SY0-701 | Governance, risk, and compliance**

Security requirements come from laws, regulations, contracts, standards, business needs, and risk decisions. Requirements vary by country, sector, data type, and the organization's role.

## 1. Regulatory requirements

Regulations may mandate protection of specific information, reporting, retention, access controls, or audits. Examples often discussed in security programs include:
- **HIPAA** in the United States, covering privacy and security requirements for applicable protected health information and covered entities/business associates.
- **SOX (Sarbanes-Oxley Act)**, which includes requirements related to financial reporting and internal controls for covered organizations.
- **GLBA (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act)**, with requirements relevant to certain financial institutions and customer information.
- **PCI DSS**, an industry payment-card data security standard rather than a general government statute.
- Data-protection regimes such as the EU's GDPR, where applicable.

The exact scope and duties depend on the law, regulation, contract, organization, and data. Do not assume every law applies to every organization in the same way.

## 2. Legal and contractual considerations

Legal considerations can include:
- Breach disclosure and notification duties.
- Privacy and data-subject rights.
- Data retention, deletion, and legal holds.
- Contractual security commitments.
- Intellectual property and confidentiality.
- Cross-border transfers and jurisdiction.
- Evidence preservation and cooperation with investigations.

Security teams should coordinate with qualified legal/compliance staff when interpreting obligations.

## 3. Industry-specific security

Different sectors have different operational risks:
- Healthcare must protect patient information while supporting clinical access.
- Financial services must protect transactions and personal financial data.
- Energy and utilities may operate critical operational technology.
- Government and defense organizations may handle classified or controlled information.
- Retail and payment environments may handle cardholder data.

A control suitable for one sector may not be sufficient for another because the threat model, availability requirements, and legal obligations differ.

## 4. Geographic scope

Security and privacy obligations can be local, regional, national, or global. An organization operating across jurisdictions may need to reconcile multiple requirements concerning data storage, access, notification, and retention.

Geographic security controls might also apply to location-restricted services or sensitive facilities. The organization should document where data is stored, where it is accessed, which service providers process it, and what jurisdictional restrictions apply.

## 5. Consequences of non-compliance

Potential consequences include:
- Regulatory fines or legal action.
- Contractual penalties or loss of business.
- Loss of licenses or permission to operate.
- Required remediation or independent monitoring.
- Reputation damage and loss of customer trust.
- Increased insurance or operating costs.

Compliance is necessary when applicable, but checking a box does not guarantee an effective security posture. Controls should reduce real risk as well as satisfy requirements.
